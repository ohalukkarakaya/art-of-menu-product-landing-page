Restaurant inventory management, done properly.

Restaurant inventory management means knowing what you have, what you should have used and what you actually used, then closing the gap between the last two. It rests on regular counts, costed recipes, par levels and first-in, first-out storage.

In short

Restaurant inventory management is the routine of counting stock, costing recipes, setting par levels and rotating stock first in, first out, so food cost matches sales. The core check compares what your recipes say you used with what the count shows. ArtOfMenu's Smart plan uses recipe-based stock to mark items sold out when an ingredient runs out, and adds sales reports.

Why inventory decides your food cost

Food cost percentage is the cost of food used divided by food sales. The cost of food used is opening stock plus purchases minus closing stock. Without an accurate count at both ends, that number is a guess, and every pricing decision built on it carries the same error.

Set up for counting

A count is only quick if the storeroom is arranged for it. Lay out the count sheet in the same order as the shelves, from the walk-in to dry storage to the bar. Use one unit per item and stick to it: count olive oil by the bottle every time, never by the case one week and the bottle the next.

Count high-value and fast-moving items, such as proteins, dairy and spirits, every week. Count everything once a month. Have the same two people count, on the same day and at the same time, before deliveries arrive.

Cost every recipe

A recipe card lists every ingredient in a dish with its quantity and cost. Use the usable yield, not the purchase weight. A case of whole fish or a bunch of herbs loses weight in trimming, so the real cost per ounce is higher than the invoice suggests.

Recheck the cards whenever an invoice price moves noticeably. A recipe card is only useful while its prices are current.

Set par levels

A par level is the amount of an item you want on hand to last until the next delivery, plus a safety buffer. A simple formula: average daily usage × days between deliveries + safety stock.

If you use 12 lb of chicken thighs a day, get deliveries every 3 days and want one day of buffer, your par is 12 × 3 + 12 = 48 lb. When you order, order up to par: with 18 lb on the shelf, order 30. Review pars monthly and before the season turns, because opening the terrace in May changes your usage.

Store first in, first out

FIFO means the oldest stock is used first. Put new deliveries behind existing stock, label every container with the date it arrived or was prepped and check dates on every shift. It reduces spoilage and keeps food safe, and it makes the walk-in easier to count.

Track waste and variance

Keep a waste sheet in the kitchen with the item, quantity, reason (spoiled, over-prepped, dropped, sent back) and who logged it. Two weeks of honest entries show patterns nobody notices day to day.

Then compare theoretical usage with actual usage. Theoretical usage is items sold multiplied by recipe quantities. Actual usage is opening stock plus purchases minus closing stock. The difference, called variance, comes from portioning, waste, theft or counting errors. Investigate the largest variances by value first.

Keep the menu in step with stock

A guest who orders a dish and hears ten minutes later that it is gone has a worse evening, and the server has an awkward conversation. When an ingredient runs out, every dish that uses it should come off the menu at once.

ArtOfMenu's Smart plan does this with recipe-based stock: link each item to its ingredients, and when one runs out, every item made with it shows as sold out on its own. Its weather-aware order also moves items that are about to run out further down the menu, and sales reports help you see what moved and plan purchases.

Spreadsheet or software?

A spreadsheet works for one location with a stable menu: one tab for the count, one for recipe costs and one for par levels. Software starts to earn its cost when you run several branches, change the menu often or want the team to count on a phone. Either way, the weekly routine is what keeps the numbers right.

Set up inventory control in seven steps

  1. List every item

    Write down everything you buy, grouped by storage area, with one counting unit for each.

  2. Do a full opening count

    Count everything before service on a fixed day and record the value at current invoice prices.

  3. Cost your recipes

    Build a recipe card for each dish with quantities, yields and ingredient costs.

  4. Set par levels

    Use daily usage × days between deliveries + safety stock, and always order up to par.

  5. Label and rotate

    Date every delivery and prep container, and store new stock behind old.

  6. Log waste daily

    Record what is thrown away and why, on a sheet kept where the waste happens.

  7. Compare and correct

    Each week, compare theoretical with actual usage and act on the largest gaps first.

Frequently asked questions

What is restaurant inventory management?

It is the process of tracking the food and drink you buy, store and use, so you order the right amounts, waste less and know your real food cost. It covers counting, recipe costing, par levels, stock rotation and waste tracking.

How often should a restaurant do inventory?

Count high-value and fast-moving items, such as proteins, dairy and spirits, weekly. Do a full count of everything at least monthly, on the same day and at the same time, before deliveries arrive.

How do I calculate par levels?

Multiply average daily usage by the number of days between deliveries, then add safety stock. When ordering, order the difference between your par level and what you have on hand.

What is the FIFO method in a restaurant?

First in, first out: older stock is used before newer stock. Store new deliveries behind existing ones and label everything with a date. It reduces spoilage and keeps food safe.

Can I manage restaurant inventory in Excel?

Yes, for a single location with a stable menu. Use one sheet for counts, one for recipe costs and one for par levels. Software becomes more useful with several branches or a menu that changes often.
