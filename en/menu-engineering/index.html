Menu engineering, explained step by step.

Menu engineering is a method for deciding what to keep, reprice, reposition or remove on your menu by comparing each item's popularity with its contribution margin. You can do it with a spreadsheet and last month's sales.

Get started See how it works
In short

Menu engineering analyzes each menu item on two measures: how popular it is and how much it earns per sale after food cost, known as contribution margin. Items fall into four groups, stars, plowhorses, puzzles and dogs, from the matrix Michael Kasavana and Donald Smith published in 1982. Each group calls for a different action. ArtOfMenu's Smart plan adds sales reports to work from.

The two numbers that matter

Contribution margin is the selling price minus the food cost of one portion. If a risotto sells for $22 and its ingredients cost $6, its contribution margin is $16. Use the price before sales tax or VAT.

Popularity is the item's share of sales within its category. Compare mains with mains and desserts with desserts, never across categories, because a guest who orders a main is choosing among mains.

The menu engineering formula

In the original method, an item counts as popular if its share of sales in its category is at least 70% of an equal share. With 10 mains, an equal share is 10%, so a main is popular if it accounts for 7% or more of main-course sales. The 70% line stops items from being labeled unpopular just for being slightly below average.

For margin, compare each item's contribution margin with the weighted average for its category: the total contribution margin of the category divided by the total number of items sold in it.

The menu engineering matrix: four quadrants

The labels sound blunt, but they make menu meetings faster, because each one comes with a clear default action.

  • Stars: high popularity, high margin. Keep them visible, keep quality consistent and test small price rises.
  • Plowhorses: high popularity, low margin. Raise the price a little, trim the portion cost or pair them with a high-margin side.
  • Puzzles: low popularity, high margin. Move them to better spots, rename them, add a photo or have staff recommend them.
  • Dogs: low popularity, low margin. Remove them, unless they serve a clear purpose, such as a children's dish or the vegetarian option a group needs.

A quick worked example

Say your dessert section sold 400 portions last month across five desserts. An equal share is 20%, so the popularity line is 14%, or 56 portions. The tiramisu sold 120 at a $7 margin. The lemon tart sold 40 at a $9 margin. The weighted average margin across all five desserts comes to $6.50.

The tiramisu is a star: popular and above the average margin. The lemon tart is a puzzle: it earns well but sits below the popularity line, so it needs better placement, a photo or a server's recommendation before you consider removing it.

Where layout comes in

The spreadsheet tells you what to change, and the layout is where much of the change happens. Puzzles in particular are often a visibility problem. Guests tend to notice the first and last items in a section, items with a photo and items set apart with a box or extra space.

Describe dishes in concrete terms, and avoid lining prices up in a neat column, which invites guests to scan for the cheapest. On a QR menu, the top of each section is the spot to give your stars and puzzles.

What a digital menu adds

Classic menu engineering only sees what was sold, and every change to a printed menu means a reprint. On a digital menu you can move a puzzle up its section, add a photo or rename it today, then compare its sales with the weeks before.

If orders rise, the problem was visibility. If they do not, look at what guests see on the item: the price, the description or the portion in the photo. On ArtOfMenu's Smart plan, sales reports show the before and after, and the menu order also adjusts itself to sales and to what guests look at.

Menu engineering and pricing

Repricing is one of the main actions the matrix suggests, especially for plowhorses. Make changes in small steps and watch whether popularity holds.

ArtOfMenu's Smart plan adds daily pricing: each evening it sets tomorrow's prices from the branch location, weather, special days and expected demand, inside the minimum and maximum you set per item. Menu engineering helps you choose those limits. A star can often carry a wider range, while a plowhorse that guests compare with other places may need a narrow one.

Common mistakes

Most menu engineering errors come from the data that goes into the matrix.

  • Using last year's recipe costs after supplier prices have moved
  • Comparing items across categories
  • Analyzing a period with a holiday or closure that distorts sales
  • Changing price, position and description at once, so you cannot tell what worked
  • Removing a dog that a regular group orders every week without asking why

How to do menu engineering in seven steps

  1. Pick a period

    Use four to eight normal weeks of sales. Leave out holidays and closures, or analyze them separately.

  2. Cost every recipe

    Work out the food cost of one portion of each item, including garnishes and included sides, at current supplier prices.

  3. Count items sold

    Pull the number sold for each item from your POS, grouped by category.

  4. Calculate contribution margin

    For each item, subtract the portion cost from the price before tax, then multiply by items sold for its total contribution.

  5. Set the two thresholds

    Popularity: 70% of an equal share in the category. Margin: total category contribution divided by total items sold.

  6. Sort into four groups

    Mark each item as a star, plowhorse, puzzle or dog, and choose one action for each.

  7. Change, then measure again

    Make the changes, leave them for at least a month and repeat the analysis with the new sales.

Frequently asked questions

What is menu engineering?

It is a way of analyzing a menu by each item's popularity and contribution margin, then deciding what to promote, reprice, reposition or remove. Michael Kasavana and Donald Smith set it out in 1982, and it is still widely taught in hospitality courses.

What is the menu engineering formula?

Contribution margin equals selling price minus food cost per portion. An item is popular if its sales share in its category is at least 70% of an equal share. It is high-margin if its contribution margin is above the category's weighted average.

What are stars, plowhorses, puzzles and dogs?

Stars are popular and profitable. Plowhorses are popular but earn little. Puzzles earn well but sell little. Dogs sell little and earn little. Each needs a different action: protect, reprice, reposition or remove.

How often should I do menu engineering?

Every quarter is a practical rhythm, plus after any major supplier price change or menu update. If you run seasonal menus, analyze each one at the end of its season.

Can I do a menu engineering worksheet in Excel?

Yes. A spreadsheet with item, category, items sold, price, portion cost and contribution margin is enough. Add two formulas for the thresholds and one column for the quadrant, then refresh it each quarter with new sales and costs.
